# Obstacle-aware three-robot formation

The current desktop launch runs as `robot-formation.service`. Check it with `systemctl --user status robot-formation.service`, restart with `systemctl --user restart robot-formation.service`, or stop with `systemctl --user stop robot-formation.service`. The service keeps Gazebo independent of temporary command sessions.

Run `bash Multi_ROBOTS/launch/launch_gazebo.sh` from this directory. Requires ROS 2 Jazzy, Gazebo Harmonic, ros_gz_bridge, and CPU PyTorch in `.venv`. Stop by closing Gazebo or pressing Ctrl+C in the launcher terminal.

The gold primary is 1.7 times the followers' linear dimensions. The arena contains six red blocks and perimeter walls. `arena.pgm` and `arena.yaml` form a surveyed occupancy map; `arena.json` describes obstacle geometry and patrol waypoints. This is a known-map simulation, not SLAM. Lidar detections are additionally included in the primary's route planning to account for map/localization discrepancies.

Only the primary has a 360-degree lidar and RGB camera. Camera processing produces three red-obstacle occupancy cues, not semantic recognition or depth. Each robot has odometry and a contact bumper. The followers receive primary perception through communication, rather than subscribing to primary sensor topics.

Three independent ROS nodes run in one process. ROS 2/DDS uses reliable, depth-3 JSON messages with version `formation/1`. `/formation/primary` carries sequence number, simulation timestamp, 0.8-second TTL, pose, trail, lidar points, image cues, and peer poses at 10 Hz. `/formation/feedback` carries follower poses, rewards, collision/stuck state, selected weights, formation error, and training loss. Followers reject out-of-order/expired messages and stop on stale communication or odometry. This is a local simulation protocol; it does not emulate radio interference or bandwidth limits. Use ROS domain 47 and Gazebo partition `rl_three_robot_formation` when inspecting the running system.

The primary follows A* routes, responds to lidar clearance and camera cues, and slows when followers lag. Followers track positions along the communicated trail with opposite lateral offsets. Each follower uses an ANN feature encoder and a four-head Transformer over six observation frames, followed by a DQN head. It selects one of six discrete weight vectors combining route tracking, formation tracking, recovery, and stop commands. Replay-based Double DQN updates run online; target weights synchronize every 100 steps and checkpoints save to `follower_1_transformer.pt` and `follower_2_transformer.pt`. Initial weights favor route tracking; these are online learners, not converged pretrained obstacle-avoidance policies.

Reward: five times clipped formation progress, minus 0.02 times formation error, plus 0.15 inside 0.3 m, minus 8 for contact and minus 2 for being stuck. Stuck detection uses two seconds of commanded motion with less than 0.08 m displacement. Contact/stuck states trigger recovery independently of neural selection. A separate predictive clearance check limits unsafe motion. Avoidance is hybrid planning and learned expert weighting, not end-to-end learned vision control or a collision-free guarantee.

Read `controller.log` for sensor counts, primary positions, follower error, selected weights, negative feedback counts, and training loss. `bridge.log` and `gazebo.log` contain transport and simulator diagnostics. Run `PYTHONPATH=Multi_ROBOTS/scripts .venv/bin/python -m unittest discover -s Multi_ROBOTS/tests -v` for sensor-placement, patrol-clearance, and negative-reward learning checks.

Both followers monitor their Gazebo world positions. A follower outside 0.5 m formation tolerance with less than 0.08 m motion over 3 seconds triggers a safe simulation pose recovery, with an 8-second cooldown. Recovery commands use XYZ with roll=pitch=0 and yaw toward the formation target, converted to a quaternion for `/world/formation/set_pose`. Candidate positions are checked against the map, shared lidar, and peer footprints. Recovery skips when no clear position exists. Requests, service confirmations, and counts appear in `controller.log` and feedback. These are simulation teleport resets, not physical robot motion commands. World-position odometry provides simulation ground truth; hardware would require a localization system.
